We consider the unconstrained optimization problem
\begin{equation}
    \underset{x \in \bbR^{n}}{\mathrm{minimize}} \quad f(x),
    \label{prob:main_problem}
\end{equation}
where $f\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a continuously differentiable nonconvex function, and $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the optimization variable.
We focus on settings where objective function evaluations, especially the function values, are contaminated by bounded and non-diminishing numerical noise~\citep{sunTrustRegionMethod2023}.
Specifically, only inexact function values $\overline{f}(x)$ are available for optimization.
Such inaccuracies are unavoidable in many realistic scenarios, including finite-precision arithmetic, simulation-based evaluations, and stochastic approximations~\citep{shiNoiseTolerantQuasiNewtonAlgorithm2022,moreEstimatingComputationalNoise2011,caflischMonteCarloQuasiMonte1998}.

In the absence of noise, \MPCchanged{quasi-Newton and regularized quasi-Newton methods can solve the large-scale problem~\eqref{prob:main_problem}, and their theoretical guarantees and practical efficiency have been well established.
For their success, the accuracy of function values is crucial.
Accurate function values are used to determine whether a step is accepted or rejected, and to adjust the step size or regularization parameter~\citep{2020SciPy-NMeth,Okazaki2007libLBFGS,uedaConvergencePropertiesRegularized2010,uedaRegularizedNewtonMethod2014,kanzowRegularizationLimitedMemory2023}.
When function values are contaminated by numerical noise, these mechanisms become unreliable, and the algorithms may lose their theoretical guarantees, behave erratically, or terminate prematurely with unreliable solutions.}

In the presence of noise, \MPCchanged{there are mainly two approaches to address function-value errors.
The first is to use a noise-tolerant methods that account for bounded errors such as relaxed Armijo tests~\citep{shiNoiseTolerantQuasiNewtonAlgorithm2022,paquetteStochasticLineSearch2020}.
The second is to avoid function values entirely~\citep{Gratton08022024,grattonOFFOMinimizationAlgorithms2023}.
However, as we will detail in \cref{sec:related_work}, the part of their theory does not cover our nonconvex setting, and totally discarding function values can limit practical efficiency when reliable function values are available.}

These observations naturally motivate us to design noise-tolerant quasi-Newton methods that use function values when they are reliable and use noise-aware strategies when they are not.
\MPCchanged{Natural idea is to combine the former with the latter, switching between them.
The key difficulty of this approach is how to determine when to switch between the two methods while maintaining convergence guarantees and practical efficiency.
If we overlook the noise, we may accept unreliable steps and lose convergence guarantees, being unstable or even diverging.
If we are too conservative, we may reject reliable steps and lose practical efficiency, slowing down the practical convergence speed.
Achieving a balance between these two is crucial for designing effective methods.}

In this work, we propose a noise-tolerant regularized quasi-Newton method.
Our approach combines Armijo-type line search with quadratic regularization~\citep{uedaRegularizedNewtonMethod2014,kanzowRegularizationLimitedMemory2023}
and Objective-Function-Free Optimization (OFFO) inspired strategy~\citep{Gratton08022024,grattonOFFOMinimizationAlgorithms2023}.
When a sufficient decrease in function value is observed, the method behaves similarly to standard quasi-Newton methods with line search, preserving its practical efficiency.
When numerical noise dominates, we introduce regularization on the approximate Hessian to ensure numerical stability, which yields convergence properties similar to those of OFFO methods, particularly AdaGrad-Norm~\citep{wardAdaGradStepsizesSharp2020}.

\MPCchanged{The key methodological advance is the carefully designed, error-model-based switch between unregularized quasi-Newton steps and OFFO-inspired regularized steps.
A relaxed Armijo condition alone may accept steps without sufficient decrease in the true objective, so its decrease estimates need not establish convergence to arbitrarily small first-order tolerances.
Our switch allows another unregularized step only after the observed objective decrease is large enough for the relaxation used at the preceding unregularized iteration.
This leads to a flexible and adaptive switching strategy that can exploit reliable function while maintaining convergence guarantees under persistent function-value noise.
Together, these estimates yield an $\order{\varepsilon^{-2}}$ iteration bound, preserving the optimal worst-case order for smooth nonconvex optimization under the problem setting of persistent function-value errors and exact gradients.}

We also conducted extensive numerical experiments on the CUTEst benchmark collection.
We compare the analyzed method, a practical variant incorporating approximation and restart mechanisms, and several baseline methods we have mentioned so far.
We examined function-value noise settings as the theory aligned one, joint function-and-gradient noise as an extended practical setting, and reduced precision settings as a realistic scenario.
Almost all of the experiments show that our method is faster in terms of oracle calls and elapsed time, and is more robust to noise than the baselines.
We also considered error-bound misspecification to assess the robustness of the method.
A fixed-iteration timing experiment shows lower overhead than the regularized L-BFGS baselines, and a separate time-to-solution comparison shows favorable elapsed-time performance at this precision and tolerance.

\begin{MPCchange}
We next compare our method with related quasi-Newton and OFFO methods, whose theoretical guarantees are summarized in \cref{tab:related_work_comparison}.

The first three methods in the table are standard quasi-Newton and regularized Newton-type methods.
The Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (BFGS) method and its Limited-memory variant (L-BFGS) are among the most efficient and widely used algorithms to solve the problem~\eqref{prob:main_problem}~\citep{liuLimitedMemoryBFGS1989,nocedal1999numerical}.
It generally employs line search procedures that enforce the Wolfe conditions to guarantee sufficient descent and the positive definiteness of the Hessian approximation.
Regularized Newton methods attain an $\order{\varepsilon^{-2}}$ iteration bound with accurate function values~\citep[Theorem~4.1]{uedaConvergencePropertiesRegularized2010}, while the regularized L-BFGS method \citep{kanzowRegularizationLimitedMemory2023} establishes global convergence to first-order stationarity.
% ここにどのようにfunction valueを使うかの説明を入れる。
As discussed, their theoretical guarantees rely on accurate function values, and do not cover function-value errors.
Their practical performance may degrade in noisy environments.

The next one is the noise-tolerant quasi-Newton (NTQN) method \citep{shiNoiseTolerantQuasiNewtonAlgorithm2022}.
NTQN uses a noise-tolerant line search and a lengthening procedure to stabilize curvature information under bounded errors in function and gradient evaluations.
The NTQN analysis allows bounded gradient errors and establishes convergence to a noise-dependent neighborhood for strongly convex objectives~\citep[Theorem~3.5]{shiNoiseTolerantQuasiNewtonAlgorithm2022}.
Its practical line search adds twice the absolute function-error bound to the Armijo right-hand side after an unsuccessful initial trial~\citep[Section~4.2]{shiNoiseTolerantQuasiNewtonAlgorithm2022}.
Our relaxation instead follows a hybrid absolute-relative error model and is recomputed at each trial point.

The next one is the OFFO method.
OFFO methods obtain convergence guarantees without evaluating function values~\citep{Gratton08022024,grattonOFFOMinimizationAlgorithms2023}.
They attain an $\order{\varepsilon^{-2}}$ iteration bound for smooth nonconvex objectives, but cannot use function-value information to guide the steps.

Lastly, our method combines the strengths of quasi-Newton and OFFO methods.
Our method instead uses inexact function values to permit unregularized quasi-Newton steps when progress is certified and retains this worst-case order under persistent function-value errors.
\end{MPCchange}
